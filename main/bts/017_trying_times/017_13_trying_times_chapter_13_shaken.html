-13-Shaken

Tess Monaghan wasn't really sure how things had gotten to this point. Natalie Rubin - a beautiful, Jewish wife and mother - had disappeared with her three children, leaving her husband Mark devastated and bewildered. He turned to Tess for help, and, from the moment of their first meeting, she had a hunch she knew what drove Natalie away. A reserved, severe Orthodox Jew, Mark's love for his wife seemed to contain a disturbing desire to control her. Yet, nothing with this case was what it seemed. Expecting to simply track down a runaway bride, Tess had instead discovered that Natalie's departure may have had more to do with desertion than escape. Stumbling her way through a web of greed, deceit and revenge, she didn't have enough fingers and toes to count the people who could be involved. Everyone she talked to seem to be playing an angle, and Natalie seemed to be at the center of all of it.

Adding to the strangeness, Tess was discovering her initial impression of her client was far from accurate. She had spent much of the day in Rubin's pale green Jaguar as the two rode out to the quiet little town of Grantsville, three hours west of Baltimore. Their goal was to speak to a man - an ex-con Mark had crossed paths with when he participated in a prison outreach program - whose name kept coming up in the investigation. She had been prepared for a tedious trip, at best, but the lengthy journey had actually revealed a side of Rubin she never expected. Effusive and even funny, he was nothing like the man she'd met that first day. Now he was trying to impress her with his knowledge of geography, saying they could see part of three different states from where they stood on the old farmhouse porch, waiting for the owner to return and answer their questions. Tess never got the chance to respond, as the homeowner returned mumbling something about how they were on his property, so he had a right to do what he was about to do. Then he raised the shotgun he was carrying and Tess instinctively reached for the gun on her hip. As quick as she was, Rubin was faster, producing a gun of his own...

And then I was interrupted when an enormous canine head landed in my lap.

"Would you get off me, you big oaf?" I said to Winzig.

Needing both hands to deal with the situation, I was forced to put the Laura Lippman novel aside. Even then, it was no small feat.

"Aww, he likes you," Danni smiled.

"I like him, too," I grunted through the effort of pushing the big Dane back, "but he's cutting off the circulation to my foot."

Meanwhile, Tramp was bounding around behind his larger companion, trying to find a way to squeeze in for some attention of his own.

Once I got free, I snatched up the book and stood before either of them could pin me down again. I read the next few paragraphs - enough to know that Tess and her client emerged in one piece - then put the book back down. My sandals clapped against the soles of my feet as I stepped over to the rail of the second story deck with both dogs in tow.

"Why are you hounding me when she's still sitting?" I asked them, pointing at Danni.

Tramp followed my finger, then turned and attacked like he hadn't seen her in months.

"Ahh!" she yelled as she hurriedly rolled off the wicker lounge chair, tossing her own book in the process.

Winzig, meanwhile, planted himself at my side and gave me his sad face.

"You're pathetic," I said as I reached down and scratched behind his ear.

He didn't appear to be offended.

I looked out over the vast expanse of rippling, wind-contoured sand to the seemingly tranquil sea and the horizon beyond. The briny sea air carried with it the aroma of garlic and rosemary wafting from a grill upwind... and I was getting hungry.

Long Beach Island - commonly known as LBI - certainly lacks the exotic feel of a secluded resort in the Caribbean, but it's quiet and picturesque. Most importantly for us, it was easily doable. No need to worry about flights or figure out whose car was where or the fact that neither of us had enough of anything for a week long trip. Staying close meant we were able to stop home, pack properly and condense ourselves and our canine companions into one vehicle. It also helped that I have a friend who owns a summer house here, so the arrangements were easy. It was too early in the season for him to be using it, and he assured me the dogs were no problem.

If he'd known Winzig was the size of a horse, he might have had second thoughts. But he didn't ask, and I didn't offer.

While lodging and transportation were non-issues, there had been a few hurdles we had to clear to get free. First, I had Isa to deal with. While I admit I don't actually know what private air travel should cost, I knew it had to be more than the pittance she put on the invoice. No matter, I had other plans. I tore the invoice up, and we hammered out an arrangement that worked for both of us.

Then came the women of RSI. As word spread that Danni and I were plotting an escape, my phone started to chirp incessantly. By some mysterious occurrence (it was Emma), our destination had also been leaked. Once they all knew we were coming home first, the demands poured in. Though the specifics were somewhat different the texts all amounted to the same message.

Don't take Danni away until we see her!

And they meant it, too. Despite the fact that we were very late getting back to the facility, the entire RSI roster was present to welcome their leader back like some long lost hero.

As the makeshift celebration carried on, Emma found me on the edge of the fray.

"So?" she asked.

"We're going away together, aren't we?"

"That's all I'm gonna get?"

"What do you want? A medal?" I teased.

"Hmmph," she grunted.

It almost made me laugh out loud. Despite her well-known activism, righteous indignation on a personal level was not her thing, and it looked terrible on her.

"You were right," I admitted, "and I knew it as soon as I saw her. So, thank you."

"My pleasure," she smiled. "I owed you one, anyway."

That was nonsense. We didn't have the kind of relationship where you keep score. But I let it slide.

"So, now what?" she asked.

"Now, we go on vacation."

"You think it's gonna work, this time?"

"I think we're going to find out."

And we did. We had spent this week simply learning to be together. It sounded strange, considering how long we've known each other and how tightly our lives have become intertwined. Events, training, near death experiences... there was always something pushing or pulling us in one direction or another. But this week had given us the chance to shut it all down and find out what we were when it was just the two of us. To be together for no other reason than because we wanted to. Could we do it, or did we need the craziness to bond us?

We discovered that, not only could we do it, but we loved it. And now, we didn't want it to end.

"Home tomorrow," Danni said from behind me, with a little note of sadness in her voice.

"Not much choice," I shrugged. "Steph and the Em and Em girls aren't paid enough for this."

Em and Em was my personal term for the pairing of Emma and Emily VanCamp. While the RSI roster was loaded with smart, capable women, they were the two we could always rely on to rise to the occasion. Our lengthy absences during the Powers debacle had forced us to call on them to back Steph up. To no one's surprise, they came through in every way.

The experience raised them up a bit in the eyes of their teammates, and I've been happy to treat them that way.

After all... when you have natural leaders like that at your disposal, you make use of them. Not to mention that a good business owner always keeps one eye on the future.

"I don't think you should call them that," Danni said. "Not to their faces, anyway."

"Not yet, no. But I think I can eventually get it to stick."

"Good luck with that."

I shrugged. She was probably right, but it wasn't going to stop me from trying.

"How's the head?" I asked.

One of the things that got lost in the emotion of our little airport reunion was the remarkable recovery Danni had made in just the single week we'd been apart. The visible injuries had healed considerably, and she said the headaches were fewer and less severe. Of course, none of that stopped me from calling Trish while we were still in the air to get a detailed treatment protocol. Turns out there was little left to do but give her time. Another week here at the shore had further reduced the apparent stuff to little more than faded bruising, and the headaches were nearly gone.

"Feeling good and hard as a rock," she reported.

I think she was trying to make fun of her occasional obstinacy, but she was playing to the wrong crowd.

"You wasted that one on me," I said. "No one's skull is as thick as mine."

A moment of silence passed.

"You're not gonna argue?" I asked.

"When you're right, you're right," she said.

Yeah... things are pretty much back to normal.

Tired of smelling food I wasn't eating, I started up our own grill. Then we headed inside to retrieve the ingredients I had prepared in advance. I pulled a plastic bag from the refrigerator and emptied the contents into a large bowl. Chicken, marinated in lemon juice, olive oil, garlic, paprika and other seasonings.

And it looked like something terrible had happened to it.

Though it was still raw, the acid from the lemon juice had turned the meat white and the marinade itself was a murky, dirty looking blend.

"That's gross," Danni observed casually.

I couldn't disagree. The mixture looked just as disgusting as it always does.

"Have faith," I responded.

I put the chicken on - the oil and citrus juice blend causing a small flare up as I set each piece down - then went back inside for the salad and corn. Jersey corn, of course, from Foster's Farm Market in Beach Haven.

The chicken was done within a few minutes, and I served it with the salad. Then, I reduced the heat and put the corn on the grill with the husks still on.

"Won't that catch fire?" Danni asked.

"No."

A piece of corn silk ignited and disappeared in a puff of smoke.

"Yes," she countered.

I gestured toward the table and said, "Shut up and eat."

The previously unattractive chicken turned out just as tasty as usual, and Danni came up with the excellent idea to cut it up and add it to the salad. By the time we'd finished with that, the corn was ready - the husks dried and blackened, but the kernels inside steamed to perfection. Along with some freshly brewed iced tea, the whole meal was an unqualified success.

After a quick cleanup, we retired to the deck once more. The two of us stood quietly at the rail for a long while, listening to the sound of the waves breaking on the shore and feeling the warm, gentle breeze on our skin.

Silence was something that had always passed easily between us, but this time together had made us experts at it.

"I don't want to go back," she said.

"Neither do I," I agreed.

The conversation stopped there, both of us thinking different thoughts.

My best guess was that Danni was thinking about training schedules and workout programs and all the things she'd have to deal with when the trip ended.

I was thinking about causing trouble.

Twenty yards away, a herring gull suddenly dropped down and snatched a piece of tidal debris from the beach. Realizing its prey wasn't food, the bird quickly dropped it back to the sand. Then it swooped around and dove again, grabbing the exact same piece of debris.

Seagulls aren't very smart.

I was about to deliberately start an argument with a woman I had never seen back down from anything.

It's possible the seagull may be smarter than I am.

"When we get back," I began, "I think we need to put this... us... on the back burner, for a little while."

Her face changed instantly. Gone was the wistful longing for a few more days of relaxation. Replaced by a mix of disappointment and anger. After a very happy week together, she was wounded by the idea that I wanted to give the relationship anything less than my full commitment.

And she was pissed about it.

"Why?" she snapped.

"Because you need to focus on your training."

"I can train and have a life, too!"

"I know, but..."

"But, nothing! The two things have nothing to do with each other!"

"They did last time."

"Yeah, well... that was last time!"

"Oh... well... now I'm convinced."

She sighed, then tried to calm herself as she searched for a better way to make her point.

When she spoke again, she seemed to have accompished one of those two things.

"What happened before was a problem," she said, "but that was before."

"Repeating the same weak argument in a more patient tone doesn't really change anything," I said.

"Shut up. I'm working on it."

"Take your time. We've got all day."

She was quiet for a long time. There was no urgency in the silence. Despite my attempt to be a smartass, we did have all day. And I would wait as long as she needed.

I didn't really mean what I was saying. I had enjoyed every moment of this week, and I didn't want to take a chance on letting what we had slip away again. Besides, it was ridiculous to think we could somehow suspend what was happening so Danni could concentrate on her training. For a fighter, training never stops. If we press pause every time she needs to focus, we'll never push play.

So, why was I aggravating her like this?

Because I needed her to argue. To push back at me and fully commit herself to the fight... and to open herself up, in the process.

"Things are different now," she said. "This week has been fun, and it feels like things have changed. I know we got in each other's way before. I think we're past that now. It feels like... I don't know... it feels like we're into this, now."

"I feel the same way," I said.

"Then let's not lose it," she said.

"We won't."

"How can you be so sure?"

"Because it is different. We're different. I think we both know what we want and neither of us is going to give up on it. But we can't let what happened in Melbourne happen again. We just can't."

I stopped there, making it seem as though the issue was as clear as black and white. Waiting to see if she would concede but fully expecting that she would battle on.

She didn't disappoint.

"If we really feel that way... if things really are different... then we shouldn't have the same problems again," she said. "We shouldn't be distracted the way we were before."

I smiled, wondering - not for the first time - if this is what it might be like to have a debate with myself.

"And what you're saying is ridiculous," she continued. "If this is going to work, then it just has to work. Regular events, special events, tournaments... there's always going to be something going on. We can't put everything on hold every time I have a fight coming up."

"Yeah, but what happened last time..."

"Won't happen again."

"You keep saying that, but..."

"It's not going to happen again," she said with a cold anger in her voice. "I won't let it."

It was a direct and forceful statement, and I noticed she never even glanced my way when she said it. That's because the anger wasn't aimed at me. It was a primal reaction that came from someplace much deeper than the mere desire to win an argument with me. Deeper and darker... and it was the place I needed her to go.

I pushed my real point out into the open.

"And you don't think this next time will be different from the others?"

The temperature where we were standing dropped about ten degrees, and it had nothing to do with weather.

For a moment, she didn't move. Then, I watched the pain spread across her face. It was nothing dramatic. No sudden look of horror or anguish. But her eyes started to wander... unable to focus as she dealt with a sudden onrush of thoughts and memories.

"It's going to be harder, this time," I said. "You know it is."

"Why?" she asked, even though she already knew.

"Because what happened in Melbourne is going be a distraction, all it's own."

"What do you mean?"

Rather than respond immediately, I waited. Giving us both a moment to adjust to the new direction of the conversation. Hoping that maybe she would pick up the thread on her own.

Understandably, she did not. She wanted it to stop. For the silence to continue and the topic to simply go away. I knew because that's what I would want if I were in her place.

But I wasn't going to let that happen.

"Don't play dumb," I said. "We both know what I'm talking about. I'm just asking you to say it out loud."

She looked away.

"This is stupid," she said. "I got my ass kicked, I hated it and I'm never going to let it happen again. There's nothing more to it than that."

"Yes, there is. I know there is."

She slid her eyes to me without turning her head.

"You think you know what I'm feeling?"

The tone was challenging. Like I couldn't possibly understand what was going on inside her.

It was also silly. She knew better.

"I do," I said.

"So then why do I have to say it?"

"Because Cole took something from you that night. Something more than a title. Because you have to face this, and it's just you and me here. Better now than back in the gym."

She didn't respond immediately, which told me I was on target, and that she didn't like it. But the ball was in her court now, so I waited her out.

"What do you think she took?" she asked.

I thought carefully about my response, taking the time to find the right words to sum up a complicated problem. Her performance against Cole had been disappointing and, quite frankly, dangerous. Thankfully, the physical damage was nearly healed. But we still had to deal with the emotional cost.

"I think she took a piece of who you are," I said.

More silence as she studied the beach for a while.

I turned around, pressed my back to the rail and studied her.

"One of the things I love about you is the way you hold yourself," I said. "Confident, but never cocky. You win all these belts and titles, but you don't talk about it. They're just there, and everyone knows it. You don't broadcast them because you know you don't have to."

"So?"

"So, tell me you still feel that way. That the confidence is still there."

Again, the silence descended. She kept her eyes fixed forward, but I could see enough of her face to see what was there.

Darkness. Cold. Doubt.

A young boy ran across the beach trailing a diamond-shaped, rainbow colored kite behind him. He had caught the breeze, but he wasn't feeding any line. The kite swirled around in a crazy spiral behind him, but it wasn't going up. Just as the boy ran out of view to the right, a man I presumed to be his father appeared to our left, shouting in vain for him to let out some string.

"Look, I know you don't want to do this..." I prompted.

"It was humiliating," she said, her voice so soft, it was barely a whisper.

I looked away involuntarily. It was the moment I'd been driving toward, but it was still stunning to hear the words come from her mouth.

"I knew what I needed to do," she continued. "I knew how to do it. But I just couldn't. She was faster and stronger. I couldn't do anything I wanted to do. I couldn't do anything but wait for her to end it."

She wasn't really talking to me. It was more like she was confessing her embarrassment to the ocean and letting me listen.

"I mean, how long have I been doing this now?" she said. "I can't remember ever being so...helpless. How did she do that to me?"

I turned and joined her in studying the panorama in front of us. Ribbons of pink, purple and orange rippled across the darkening sky. A couple of times this week, we had walked the few blocks west to watch the sunset from Bayview Park. Pretty, but I preferred this view - where the sky was painted by some unseen force.

This perspective was also more appropriate to the current conversation. Rather than watching the sun disappear below the horizon, here the darkness seemed to rise up and swallow the light.

"Maybe it's time for me give it up," she said.

I shook my head but stayed quiet. She needed to get through this before I could do anything, and I knew she wasn't done, yet.

"She beat me in every way there is, and she took her sweet time doing it. Just biding her time and picking her shots. She was playing with me. Like she had nothing to fear from me."

She closed her eyes and took a deep breath - fighting off a rising tide of flashbacks from a night that seemed so long ago. So much had happened, it was hard to believe it had only been a couple of weeks.

"The worst part is that I knew," she said. "Within the first few minutes, I knew she was right. I had nothing. There was nothing I could do to stop her."

The most painful part of the statement was what she didn't say. It wasn't just that she knew Cole was better that night, but that she had to fight round after round with those thoughts in her head.

Then came the question that told me we'd hit rock bottom.

"Where do I go from here?" she asked.

I knew that wasn't just depression or sadness talking. She meant it. Every minute the fight continued had been torture for her. Every blow tore away another jagged strip of her confidence, until she had nothing left. Since then, she's still been acting the part. Pretending to be who she'd always been. But, inside, her conviction was gone. That's what Cole had taken. Her faith in herself. That inner mettle that made a talented fighter into an elite warrior.

That's not an assumption on my part. I knew it for a fact because I had been through it. That's why I understood what she was feeling.

It's also why I wasn't going to let this shit stand.

"Are you done with your little pity party?" I asked.

She cut her eyes my way and glared at me.

"I just want to make sure," I said. "I don't want to interrupt."

I got nothing but the glare. I stared right back at her.

"What you're going through is nothing," I said. "It's a hiccup. A temporary setback."

She started to shake her head, as she turned again to look out into the darkness.

"You weren't ready," I continued.

"That a lame excuse," she said.

"It's not an excuse," I said. "An excuse would be that you weren't ready because you and I were being stupid. But you just said a little while ago that we're past that, right?"

She nodded grudgingly.

"So, were not talking about excuses. We're talking about what happened. Doesn't matter why. You weren't on top of your game. Like a knife that hadn't been properly sharpened. Still capable, but dull."

She rolled her eyes at the reference.

"Don't roll your eyes at me," I said. "Cheryl Cole has always been a nightmarish matchup for you. You need every ounce of your ability to deal with her, and you didn't have it that night. That razor's edge perfection you usually have wasn't there. So, of course you got beat. But there's no need to make it anything more than it was, and it sure as hell isn't a reason to quit. You're a lot better than what you showed, and you can get that edge back."

"How can you be so sure?"

"Because I'm going to be the stone you sharpen yourself on."

The statement caught her off-guard, and raw emotion was etched all over her face when she turned to me.

"I know who you are," I said steadfastly. "I understand you, and I know what you're capable of. You're not going back in that cage until I see that from you."

She shook her head lightly.

"It's not your..."

I put my palm up and gestured for her to save it.

"You think what happened that night was your fault," I said, "and I think I let you down. Truth is, it doesn't matter. Those nights in the training room were a part of who we are, and we let it fall apart. We need to get it back. If we can use it to make you better in the process, what's wrong with that?"

She looked out again. Thinking it over.

I pressed on because I wasn't making a suggestion.

"Every day, you and I are going to meet," I said, "and I'm going to put you through hell. And I will not stop until you are at the very top of your game."

She still looked unsure. Again, I understood. She was unlikely to say it out loud, but she knew she needed help if she was going to mount a comeback. But needing help wasn't her style. She didn't lean on others. She was the one they leaned on.

I went for the big finish.

"So, this guy's walking down the street..."

"Aww, Richelle..." she groaned.

"Just hear me out," I said. "He's walking down the street, and he falls in a hole. The walls are so steep, he can't get out."

I looked at her. She appeared to be studying the night sky, looking for fireflies. Or maybe she was thinking about jumping. I couldn't tell.

"You with me, so far?" I asked.

"Guy in a hole," she said. "Got it."

"So, a doctor passes by and the guy shouts up, Hey you. Can you help me out? The doctor writes a prescription, throws it down in the hole and moves on. Still with me?"

"It's complicated, but I'm hanging in there."

"OK, so... then a priest comes along, and the guy shouts up Father, I'm down in this hole, can you help me out? The priest writes out a prayer, throws it down in the hole and moves on."

I slid a half step toward her. Close enough that our arms were touching.

"Then a friend walks by," I continued. "Hey Joe, it's me, can you help me out? And the friend jumps in the hole. Our guy says, Are you stupid? Now we're both down here."

I waited until she turned to look at me before I finished.

"The friend says, Yeah, but I've been down here before, and I know the way out."

We silently held each other's gaze for a long beat.

"We're not going to dive into my past, right now. But understand that I have been where you are. Where everything you knew yesterday no longer seems certain. What happened rocked you inside as much as out. Now you feel like you're drowning in doubt."

She nodded absently. Agreeing without realizing she was doing it.

"What I'm telling you is that we're going to get you out of that, even if I have to grab you and drag you to the surface, myself."

She kept her eyes on me as she let the idea play out in her mind. Picturing the two of us, working together to hammer her back into the weapon she had been.

You and me against the world.

Then, she nodded again. More firmly, this time.

"OK," she said. "Let's do it."

It was a heartwarming moment. The two of us, agreeing to put our noses to the grindstone and push her back to where she belonged. Somebody cue the Rocky  theme and a training montage.

Only, it wasn't going to be quite that simple.

"There's something else," I said.

She sighed.

"Of course. Why wouldn't there be?"

"This one is definitely your own fault."

"Fantastic."

"Despite the things you've accomplished, you never act like you're better than anyone," I said. "You treat every fighter as your equal. Or, at least, your potential equal. And that's what makes you so good at your job. You make our girls feel like you're all in this together. That you care about their problems."

"I do care about their problems."

"I know," I said, "but what you don't realize is how much they care about yours."

That surprised her a little, and it drew a genuinely appreciative smile. Then, it shrank away as she realized where this was headed.

"I'll admit, it makes me feel good to hear you say that," she said, "but no one needs to worry about me. I can..."

"Too late," I cut in. "They rarely get the chance to repay you for the things you've done, and they're not going to pass this one up."

"But I don't want..."

"They won't care what you want," I said. "It's going to happen, so you might as well accept it."

Her gaze drifted for a bit as she tried to imagine some way I wasn't right. Then, she sighed in a combination of frustration and resignation because there was no way. When we got back, her teammates were going to be working overtime to try and pick her up. They were probably plotting as we spoke. There would be no stopping them.

"You want my advice?" I asked.

"Does it matter?"

"Don't fight it," I said. "Let them help. You've done more than you realize, and I think it will do you some good to let that selflessness flow back to you for a little while."

Her gaze drifted again, her eyes dark and serious as she thought about what I was saying.

"And when it gets to be too much," I said, "you come see me. You unload it on me, and then we'll figure it out together."

The eyes came back to me. She looked happier... and suspiciously amused.

"I like the sound of that," she said, "but now you have me wondering... how did we get from we need to put this on the back burner to you being the stone that sharpens me?"

I shrugged dismissively.

"So you didn't mean what you said about taking a break?" she asked.

I shook my head.

"I figured I could bullshit you, one last time."

"Last time?"

I shrugged again.

"Soon, I don't think you'll buy it anymore," I said.

"What does that mean?"

"It means that there's no taking breaks. That this is serious, now."

A little smile appeared on her face.

"It means I believe in us. And I think you know it."

The smile broadened slowly - one beautiful centimeter at a time - until it overwhelmed me.

I'd seen her smile a million times, but now it felt like I was seeing it again for the first time. Like every detail was somehow more pronounced and striking than ever. I saw the way the laugh lines cut into her cheeks and the way her eyes shined. I noticed how she just let the smile go - allowing it to take over her entire face. The sight was breathtaking and I wondered why it had never hit me that way before.

In that instant, I pushed everything else away. I didn't think. I didn't worry. I let my emotions carry me and simply leaned over and gently pressed my lips to hers.

Despite the fact that we'd spent nearly every second of this week together, the intimate moments had been indirect. A touch here. A look there. Little things that didn't mean much by themselves, but together gave the consistent feeling that something was happening.

As simple and spontaneous as it was, this kiss felt like the culmination of all that. Neither of us could know yet where this was all going to go, and it was much too early to start talking about committments and forevers. But it all felt much different than it had a week ago. Like we were done with trying so hard to grow this relationship because it didn't require that much effort. This week had been simple and it had been wonderful. And it had been exactly what we needed.

"What was that for?" she asked when we separated.

"No special reason," I said. "I just wanted to."
