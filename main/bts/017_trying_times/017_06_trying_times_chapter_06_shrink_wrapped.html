Author's Note: The Spenser and Susan Silverman characters belong to mystery novelist Robert B. Parker. Mr. Parker is one of my favorite authors, and his work is a major influence on the the way I write. Whether I even come close to mimicking his style is open to considerable debate, but I have chosen to use his characters in this story as something of an homage. My goal is to handle them as lightly as possible and to maintain them as Mr. Parker created them. Any failure on that front is completely my own.

-6-Shrink Wrapped

Oceanport, New Jersey - Ten days before the title fight in Melbourne

There is nothing like the first signs of spring, when the flora and fauna suddenly reappear. The muted greens and basic browns of winter are joined by pops of red and yellow and purple as life as a whole seems to renew itself. The sun is warmer and somehow brighter than it was just the day before. Splashes of pink dot the landscape as the cherry blossoms begin to bloom, and the air smells sweeter and cleaner than it has in months.

In Jersey, this phenomenon occurs three times a year. The first is usually in late March or early April. The sun comes out and the world greens up, and it's simply beautiful for a few days. Just long enough to get us thinking the days of gray skies and frigid temperatures are behind us. Then the mercury drops like a rock as winter reasserts its authority. Suddenly, everything is confused. Colorful flowers give the impression of warmth even as digits threaten to fall off due to the cold. Maybe a week after that, spring returns - even warmer and brighter than the first time. But only long enough to convince you winter is gone for good. Then, it snows. My father was known to use a few four-letter words in the presence of his children, despite a concerted effort to show restraint. But an April blizzard was the kind of thing that would drive him over the edge. Cleaning the front walk with his gloves on his hands, his fuzzy woolen hat on his head and his jacket zippered all the way to his nose, he'd mutter every curse word I'd ever heard and even introduce me to a few new ones. Now that I have my own walk to clear, I know exactly how he felt. There's nothing like the feeling you get when a foot of snow falls three days after the thermometer hits seventy-degrees. It makes you want to hit yourself in the head with the shovel and end your misery, rather than freezing to death while you use it for its intended purpose one more fucking time. But the only choice is to suffer thought it if you want to see spring return to stay a week or so later.

We were in the midst of this year's first refreeze now, which is how I found myself walking past a bed of colorful irises fighting for survival while carrying two steaming paper cups in my gloved hands. Ever been forced to experience the signs of spring while wearing a minimum of four layers of clothing? It's madness. But I was lucky. The wind was calm today and the sun was shining - factors that combined to hold the temperature just above freezing. In December, this would be considered a beautiful day. But it was far from ideal park weather, which is why I passed only a handful of people as I stepped my way along the well worn path.

I was here because I needed help. My conversation with Danni had gotten nowhere because it was crippled from the start. Every plan of attack I came up with seemed risky, at best.

We needed to talk about our relationship but that promised to be a long discussion, and I didn't want to distract her any further with the Cole bout so near.

We needed to talk about how our recent brush with death was affecting her, but that promised distraction of an even greater magnitude.

We needed to talk about her readiness for the bout, but how I could I do that without killing her confidence?

In the end, there wasn't anything left that we actually could  talk about.

As I rounded the little garden, small stone tables appeared alongside the walkway. Each was permanently topped with the instantly recognizable black and white checked pattern of a chessboard. Two stone cylinders sat beside each table, positioned as chairs for the players.

Those cylinders were all empty now, save for the one nearest me. That one was occupied by a middle-aged man of medium height and medium build. Salt and pepper hair, beard and mustache - all neatly trimmed. A pair of oval, rimless glasses perched on his angular nose giving him a soft, intelligent look.

He watched patiently as I set one of the cups on his side of the chess board, then took my place on the opposite cylinder. To be sure I hadn't confused the two, I quickly checked the side of my cup for the telltale HC marking. Coffee and hot chocolate are two very different experiences, and I didn't think he'd be too happy if he expected one and got the other.

Once I was sure I had it right, I raised my eyes and looked at my tablemate.

"Cream, two sugars," I said to him, confirming that I'd remembered how he likes his coffee.

He nodded once, but kept his expression neutral. The way you thank someone for one thing just before you give them a hard time about something else.

"You know, I have an office," he said in a firm, but casual tone. "It's got furniture and heat and little framed diplomas and certificates on the walls that remind me that I actually know what I'm doing. It's even got a little couch that people sometimes lie on. Just like in the movies."

"Maybe that's the problem," I said, trying to make light. "Maybe I don't want to fall into that movie cliche."

His expression told me I wasn't as funny as I thought I was.

"I love the outdoors as much as anyone," he said. "Though, to be honest, I love it more when the temperature is above forty degrees."

I gave a little apologetic shrug.

"But we can't keep meeting this way," he continued. "Part of the process is that you have to actually commit to being in the process."

The process he was talking about was commonly referred to as psychotherapy, but I still call it head shrinking. It was not a process I ever had any desire to go through, and it's also not something I ever would have admitted I needed on my own. Sure, the fact that I willingly spent a significant portion of my life fighting in the brutal, seedy world of underground fighting probably indicates that I'm nuts. But I was born this way, so it always seemed normal to me. The things I saw - and did - while I was part of that world eventually took a toll on me, though, and the saga in Boston brought the whole thing to a head.

It seemed minor at the time, but one of the the most important moments that came out that drama was that I was introduced to Susan Silverman. Though she was introduced as the love of Spenser's life, it didn't take long for me to realize she was much more than that. Maybe it was because she was a woman who was there for me at a time when my whole world seemed dominated by men. Maybe it was the confident, almost fearless way she carried herself. Or maybe it was just that she helped me when I desperately needed it. Whatever the reason, I came to trust her implicitly. I didn't even hold her chosen career as a psychologist against her. No doubt, she immediately realized I was a lunatic, but she never treated me like the case study I could have been. In fact, she never pushed or prodded me in any direction at all. Once - and only once - she mentioned that what was happening was likely to come crashing down on me at some point. She said that, when that happened, she wanted me to call her. Whenever it happened. Whatever time of day it was. Just call and she would help.

I didn't worry much about what she said. I was tough. Strong. I made my living with my fists, and I solved my problems by kicking their collective ass. Other people may let themselves be beaten by emotion, but that wasn't going to happen to me.

Except that it did.

When I got back home, I had the chance to really think about what had happened. My mind began to wander to places it had never gone before. I found myself involuntarily spending a lot of time thinking about what I'd done that horrible night. Not just the end result, but all the mistakes I'd made that got me there. The lies I'd been telling myself. The fantasy I was living as some sort of grand champion in a world run by criminals, murderers and thugs.

At first, I believed those thoughts were what Susan had warned me about. Doubts and insecurities were suddenly ever present, but I was managing them. Just like I said I would. Fighting them off, one at a time. I had it under control.

Then, one night, it became too much, and I quickly realized I didn't have anything under control. The thoughts and doubts and regrets came all at once. I started to see the sum total of what I'd done, and I finally admitted to myself that a woman had been made to pay for my overwhelming stupidity with her life. Instead of deflecting blame, I suddenly accepted all of it. More than I probably deserved, to be honest. But, it didn't matter. My mind flew right past the point where I could control it. For hours, I sat in the darkness, curled in a ball in the corner of my bedroom, and cried uncontrollably.

When I finally got hold of myself - in the three AM darkness of a Wednesday morning - I called Susan. As promised, she answered. And then she helped. It took the rest of the night and part of the morning before I felt ready to even stand up, but Susan stayed on the line the entire time. We talked again the next night - at a much more reasonable hour - and the night after that, and then again the night after that. It kept on that way until I finally understood that I needed real help. Not just phone conversations, but legitimate, full-blown help.

The distance between New Jersey and Massachusetts was a little too much for Susan and I to overcome, but she was still there for me. She contacted a colleague from Harvard who had later wandered south and landed in my neck of the woods. True, the fact that he moved from the beautiful and progressive state of Massachusetts to the corrupt, confused and ass-backward state of New Jersey made me question his sanity more than my own, but Susan promised that Dr. Matthew Kaplan was very good at what he did. Not that it mattered. It's not like I was doing intense research to find the most highly regarded therapist in the land. With no insurance and not a whole lot of money, I had no choices. It was only because of Susan that Dr. Kaplan agreed to see me. So, it was him or no one.

Unsurprisingly, Susan's assessment was dead-on. Despite the emotional distance I steadfastly maintain for myself, he managed to get me to trust him. His naturally studious look certainly helped. It made it appear as though he was intently interested in every word that came out of my mouth. By the end of the first session, he had convinced me to come back. Those who know me would tell you that was no small feat. By the end of the second session, I began to believe that maybe he didn't just look interested. Maybe he actually was.

"We've been over this," I said now. "I've been through the process. It helped, and I'm grateful. But I don't need all that again. I just need a little help. That's all."

"That process is how  I help people," he said. "It's called therapy, and it's what I do for a living. Went to school for it and everything."

"No kidding?" I said.

"Yeah, it's how I got the pretty little diplomas and certificates. And you know what they taught me while I was there?"

"That Freud had trouble getting dates in college?"

"That this kind of drive-by approach doesn't work," he said. "You can't just come to me with whatever's bothering you on a particular day and expect me to magically make you feel better."

"It's working, so far."

"No, it's not," he countered, "and you know it's not."

I found myself looking deliberately off into the trees, searching for anything I could use to change the conversation. At least once a week, I damn near drive off the road as I swerve to avoid a kamikaze deer running blindly into traffic. Why couldn't I find one traipsing through the woods when I needed one?

I knew he was right. I could feel that something was wrong. Not the sort of overwhelming collapse I'd experienced after Boston, but a darkness looming at the edge of my conciousness. A constant sensation that I was waiting for something terrible to happen.

"What you went through back in Boston was a very difficult thing to deal with. I've told you many times that you should not have stopped our sessions."

"Look, I just..."

"I know," he said. "You feel like you have a handle on things."

His voice was level and patient. I freely acknowledge that I am a complete pain in the ass. I'm sure I frequently make his work more difficult than it needs to be. But, despite my consistent recalcitrance, he rarely let his frustration show. It was one of the many things I liked about him, though I found it difficult to understand why he put up with me.

Truth be told, I often wonder why anyone puts up with me.

"I feel that way because you helped me," I offered.

"I appreciate you saying that, but psychology isn't a cut and dried thing. I didn't cure your illness or heal some wound. I was helping you manage, and I think I can continue to help you, if you'll let me. Given what's happened recently, I almost feel like I have to insist."

Again, I knew he was right. The darkness had started when Powers returned, but it hadn't gone away when he did. If anything, that uneasy feeling had been growing. But knowing something was wrong didn't seem to be enough to get me to seek help. In fact, quite the opposite was happening. I just wanted to shut it all down. What had happened - and, more importantly, what had happened to Danni - was behind us now. Powers was gone and Danni was safe and I just wanted to forget the whole thing. Not revisit it in forty-five minute increments.

"I understand you're trying to help," I said. "And I know you're probably right. But I'm just not ready to go through it again. Not now."

He looked at me for a moment, went to say something, then sat back and sighed - the sound long and soft and barely perceptible. It's the signature sound someone makes when frustration and resignation battle to a stalemate, leaving that person unable to decide whether to scream or surrender. I know it well because I hear it often. I have that effect on people.

He appeared momentatily lost in thought. I knew from past experience that this meant resignation had won, and he was coming to grips with giving up. Even if it was only temporary.

"Well, I can't force you. As I said, I can't help you unless you want me to. But, I'm serious... I think you should consider it."

"I will," I said solemnly. For a moment, I even thought I might have meant it, too.

He sighed again, then folded his hands on the tabletop.

"So," he said, "why am I out here freezing my butt off?"

"Because you like me so much?"

"You're pushing it."

When was I not?  I thought to myself.

"I wanted to talk to you about my friend."

He nodded.

"The one who was threatened," he said.

"Yeah. Danni."

"And how is that relationship going?"

I had told him previously about the way things were changing between Danni and I. At the time, I had used it as an example of how fine I was and how my life couldn't have been better.

I only wished I still felt that way now.

"It's not," I said. "That's why I'm here."

He laughed quickly. Like he hadn't meant to, but just couldn't help it.

"You expect me to dissect your love life for you?" he said. Then he held up the paper cup and said, "You probably should have brought a bigger cup of coffee."

I smiled and said, "No, I wouldn't put that on you. Besides..."

I trailed off, unsure of what I wanted to say.

"Besides what?" he asked.

I shifted uncomfortably, and not just because I was sitting on a glorified rock.

"I don't know. I just keep thinking, maybe she's better off. You know?"

"What makes you say that?"

"I just...I don't know."

He waited, giving me space to think things through. I tapped my gloved thumb on the tabletop for a few moments, but the thoughts that came to me weren't ones I wanted to share.

"I really don't," I concluded.

"You don't think the relationship can work?" he prompted.

"I don't know," I said again, showing a little frustration. "I don't even know why I said that. It's not what I wanted to talk about."

His eyes turned sad for a moment. Like my answer had not only been wrong, but also depressing. I just sat quietly, annoyed that I had opened this door and determined to slam it closed, again.

I guess that determination showed on my face because he let the topic drop despite the fact that he clearly didn't want to.

"What did you want to talk about?"

"I'm worried about her."

"Your friend?

"Yeah. I'm worried about how she's handling... recent events."

"I see," he said. "Has something happened?"

"Not specifically, no. She just seems a little off. Maybe I'm crazy. I don't know. But I can't help thinking this was a lot to deal with. It has to be affecting her, don't you think?"

"What I think is that it's interesting that you don't believe it has to be affecting you, too."

The angry glare I shot him spoke volumes, and he heard every word.

"I see," he said again. "So, what is it you want me to do? I can't help your friend by proxy."

Once again, he seemed to consider whether or not he should press me. Part of me recognized that I was being unfair to him. As much as I hate it, I know his work has its benefits, and I was denying him the ability to do it the way he wanted to. But I didn't have the luxury of worrying about that. Danni was running out of time, and I was running out of ideas.

"I know that," I said, "but I do think she needs help. I was just hoping you could give me some advice on how to get her to see that."

"How do know she's not aleady getting help?"

"I guess I don't," I admitted, "but, if she is, it doesn't seem to be working."

"Why do you say that?"

"I don't know. She's just... not herself."

"Have you talked to her about it?"

"I tried, but it's a little tricky," I said.

"Did you mention the idea of her seeing someone?"

I shook my head.

"I thought about it, but I know she won't go for it. I don't think there's anything I could say that would work. She's too stubborn."

"Oh, so she's like you."

You have no idea, I thought.

"And you can't try to change her mind," he continued, "because you know what her response would be."

My anger flashed again as I realized what he was driving at. It was twice as bad this time because I knew he was right. Danni would say exactly the same thing he had. That I had a lot of nerve suggesting she needed help when I'd already decided that I didn't.

"I thought we were going to move past this," I said.

"That's fine," he said, "but then I don't know why we're even here. I can't help your friend this way, and you won't let me help you."

"I guess you're right," I said darkly.

He closed his eyes and sighed deeply. The disappointment on his face was obvious, and seeing it there softened my stance.

"OK," he said with a defeated sigh. "Well, there really isn't much I can offer as far as how to talk to her. Obviously, you want to be empathetic, but if you're looking for some way to bring it up without bringing it up... there really is no way to do that."

That wasn't very helpful, but I tried not to make a face.

"If your friend decides to seek treatment, I can help," he continued. "Either with an appointment or a recommendation to see someone else. Whichever is more appropriate."

I nodded.

"And if you change your mind about talking, you know how to reach me. I'm serious. I think it would be worth it."

I nodded again.

"But, no more of these casual meetings," he said. "Next time, my office. No exceptions."

His tone was level, but firm. Serious, but not angry.

"OK," I said, "but then I'm not bringing you coffee."

He picked up his cup, tapped it twice on the table as he looked at me, then nodded his thanks again and left me there to freeze my ass off as I struggled with more problems than I'd arrived with.

Not only had I made no progress on getting Danni help, but I was now overcome by the feeling that I was doing just about everything wrong.
