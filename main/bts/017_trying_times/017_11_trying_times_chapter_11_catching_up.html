-11-Catching Up

The clock in the Subaru read ten minutes after ten as I peeled off Route 34 onto the access road for Monmouth Executive Airport. As I whipped past the tiny sign for WaWa University - yeah, you heard that right - I had to make a conscious effort to lay off the accelerator. Something about the idea of showing up at Danni's childhood home and trying to drag her out of there struck me as a difficult task. It seemed to me things would go much smoother if I managed to catch her at the airport, and the difficulties she'd been facing in Los Angeles meant I had a shot at it. Unfortunately, with a more than three hour flight ahead of me, it didn't feel like time was on my side. If I wanted to be sure to catch her, I'd need to be on my way in less than an hour. An impossibility if I was flying commercial. Check-in, bag processing, security screenings... not a chance in hell I'd make it. I assumed flying private would gain me some time, but I had no idea if it would be enough.

Following the signage, I found the main - and only - terminal at the southern tip of the airport. A simple, free-standing structure, it's painted, corrugated steel walls gave off a strictly utilitarian vibe. Likewise, the parking was a no-frills affair. There were no long-term or short-term options. Just a single lot outside the building. I dumped the car in one of the numerous empty spaces and sprinted inside, looping my purse over my shoulder as I ran. I banged through the door and skidded to a stop, and the lone blonde receptionist looked up with a start, eyeing me like the crazy woman I was.

"Can I... help you?" she asked.

Before I had the chance to respond, a familiar voice said, "I've got this one, Charlene."

Happy not to have to deal with this particular hot mess, Charlene gave me a bright smile and went back to whatever she'd been doing before I walked in.

From a small office to my right, Isabela Ortega emerged - the picture of casual professionalism in tan khakis and a simple, peach-colored blouse that brought out those liquid brown eyes I knew so well. The loose tendrils of lustrous, dark hair that framed her oval face seemed to have a mind of their own, yet somehow came together into disorganized perfection. She carried herself with the quiet confidence of someone who's seen and handled much worse than whatever you've got to throw at her, and I found myself wondering if Charlene knew where that self-assured poise came from. I certainly did. I had seen what those well-manicured hands of hers could do, and I knew the size of the heart that beat in her chest. Though she wasn't large or particularly muscular, Isa wasn't a dainty thing, either. Her clothes did a fair job of softening her hard, square frame, but the definition in her arms and shoulders revealed that she knew her way around a weight room. She also knew her way around a ring, cage, pit or any other fighting evironment you could think of. That's not something you can tell just by looking at her, but you can take my word for it.

She arched an eyebrow at my disheveled and harried appearance and said, "Got a plane to catch?"

I swear. Everyone I know thinks they're hilarious.

"Sorry," I replied. "I'm on kind of a tight schedule."

She glanced at the clock on the wall and said, "I thought you needed to be in New Orleans by two-thirty?"

"That's right," I said, "but I didn't know how long the process would be here."

"There's no process," she said. "My pilot is running through his takeoff checklist, now."

"No security?" I asked.

She shook her head.

"No metal detectors? No wands?"

More head shaking.

"You can keep your shoes on, too," she said.

As someone who is regularly subjected to pre-flight procedures that sometimes take longer than the flight itself, I was understandably skeptical. But her expression gave no indication she was messing with me. Instead, I saw the knowing look of a woman who had guided people through this type of disbelief many times before.

To punctuate her point, she gestured toward a sliding double-door at the back of the building and said, "He's gonna pick you up outside in about five minutes. You'll be in the air in ten."

I took a deep breath, then blew it out along with most of my stress. That kind of timing would put me in Louisiana nearly an hour before Danni's flight landed.

I was going to catch her.

"I sure as hell could get used to this," I said.

"That's what they all say," she said. Then her expression turned slightly sour as she added, "Then I never see them again."

"Things not going well?" I asked.

She shrugged.

"Apparently, the economic recovery isn't all it's cracked up to be. People are doing better, but the recession scared the shit out of them. They're being far more cautious with their money, and flying private just isn't in the cards," she said. "I get a fair number of people in a hurry, but, without a corporate contract, it's tough sledding. I'm hanging on, though."

"I thought you had a regular deal with that tech company. CommTech, or whatever it was. The one that moved into the fort property when it closed down."

"Budget cuts," she said with another shrug. "They keep telling me they'll be back when the money frees up. We'll have to see if I'm still around."

With my own troubles suddenly minimized, I felt a sharp pang of sadness for her. Like me, she had walked away from the old life determined to make something of herself in the real world. As far as I knew, she had done just that. Things had been tough for her at the start, but she was smart with her money - stretching it until the breaking point. Long enough for the CommTech contract to roll in and put her on steadier ground. Now, it sounded like the wheels were coming off after all that work.

"Is it really that bad?" I asked.

"You're the first customer I've had all week," she said with a sad smile, "and you're not even gonna be paying."

"The hell I'm not," I said sternly. "I called you because I knew you could help me out. Not for some sort of freebie. You bill me like anyone else. You don't owe me anything."

"I'll work something up," she said dismissively.

"No, no something," I said. "I'm not just covering your costs, or some bullshit like that. I want a full bill."

She gave me a whatever shrug, then ended the conversation by gesturing me toward her office.

"C'mon," she said. "You didn't come here to hear my sob story. Let's get you squared away."

The squaring away consisted of nothing more than a few perfunctory questions and me showing her my driver's license. It was so informal that I got the impression she was only doing it so she could say she had if she were ever asked.

The brief process left us a few minutes to chat about 'the old days,' though there was nothing there for us to mine, either. That's because Ms. Isabel Ortega had long ago gotten me to break a rule I swore was unbreakable. There were no friends in that world. Only alliances born of necessity and convenience. Those relationships were admittedly weird, and complicated to explain. Let's just say that, when you spend your life wallowing in the muck, it's not a bad idea to have people who will pull you out when it gets too deep. I had a group like that around me. People I could count on, and who knew they could count on me. In fact, they could still count on me to this day. But that was business, not friendship. Friendship in that world would be nothing but trouble, and I had done my best not to let people get too close to me. That's how it was supposed to work, anyway. But Isa's sharp wit and cool confidence had proven irresistible, and I ended up with a friend I never wanted. It had been a risky attachment, as she became much more than business to me. If something had happened to her, my reaction would have been... acute. And probably dangerous. The kind of dangerous that gets people killed. But luck was on our side, and both of us had escaped relatively intact.

I guess stupid works out, sometimes.

Anyway... she and I still spoke somewhat regularly and had already done all the reminiscing we could handle. So, the conversation quickly broke down into the two of us hurling insults at each other.

What can I say? I know what I'm comfortable with.

Before long, she was leading me out the back door to the boarding area, which was little more than a metal covering that extended off the back of the building. Like the area outside of a hotel where you can unload your luggage while protected from the elements. Only this one was plane-sized.

Standing out on the asphalt, I could see a line of propeller-driven aircraft neatly arranged to my right, while a shorter line of four jets extended left at a roughly forty-five degree angle. The arrangement reminded me of a clock face, with the first line aimed where the three would be, while the second angled toward the ten position. A taxiway ran off toward the runways in the one o'clock direction.

I focused my attention on the jets. The first three were on the smaller side and looked like they could transport a handful of passengers, at most. Farthest out, however, was a larger jet that easily dwarfed its companions, yet there was something else about it that was off. Despite its modern appearance, it looked more like a museum piece than an active aircraft. It was out of line with the others, and there was a sizable gap between it and the last of the smaller craft.

In other words, it kinda looked like it had been driven out into the middle of nowhere and left to die.

As the first of the smaller jets ramped up its engines and started to roll, I asked Isa what the story was with the big one.

"That," she said with a sigh, "is going to be the death of me."

It turned out the plane was a purchase she'd made after scoring the CommTech contract. Being a consulting firm, her new clients made a point of telling her they were were going to need to move large teams of people around the world to satisfy the needs of their own clientele. Sometimes fifty or more people traveling at once. Since her smaller aircraft couldn't accomodate anywhere near that number, she had gone in search of something bigger. When a regional carrier went belly-up in Indiana, she saw her chance and grabbed the nearly hundred passenger Boeing Business Jet for a meager sixty million dollars. Despite the fact that my eyes nearly flew out of their sockets when I heard the price, she went on to explain that it was a bargain considering that the interior was already finished. She said the plane was only a few years old, and it had probably cost upwards of eighty million when it was new. At the time, she had been thrilled to find a plane that fit her needs yet fell well within her budget. Then the contract was pulled, and her budget was shot to shit. Twenty million in savings was no longer a great deal when most of her expected income was gone and the bills still had to be paid. In essence, the plane had become a big, shiny noose around her neck.

"I can't sell the fucking thing," she said. "Everyone else is in the same situation. They're shrinking, not looking to expand."

I didn't have anything encouraging to say, so I just shook my head.

"I know what you're thinking," she said, "I should have written the cost into the contract."

"I was actually thinking you'd have been fucked, either way. They never would have signed the contract with a sixty million dollar out clause attached to it. I was also thinking that the fact that you're still in business after something like that means you must me pretty good at managing your shit."

"Yeah," she snorted. "Talk to me again in a month."

Her words made me cringe because they didn't sound like the Isa I knew. In our fighting days, odds made no difference to her. She always believed she could win. And, to be honest, I think that confidence won her a lot of fights she should have lost. Talented opponents with more than twice her experience would end up beaten at her feet. Victims of her surprising speed. Quick on her feet with bullet-fast hands, she had a talent for simply overwhelming her adversaries. Constantly attacking and filling her opponent's vision with the blur of those custom blue gloves she was so fond of. Ramping up the pressure until she forced a mistake.

It was depressing to see her displaying that same confidence now, only in reverse. Where she used to be certain of victory, now she seemed assured of failure.

Oh, and by the way... bullet-fast hands. Blue gloves.

Blue Bullet Air. Get it?

The smaller jet rolled to a stop about thirty feet from where we were standing, leaving me with the impression that my ride was here.

I looked at Isa and said, "There'd better be a bill waiting for me when I get back, or I'm kicking your ass."

A little harsh, I know, but... wait for it...

"You're looking a little old," she replied. "Do you really think you still can?"

See?

"Fuck you," I said with a smile. "You're no spring chicken, yourself."

"Always gonna be younger than you, though," she said.

I shook my head in familiar frustration.

Six months. That's the age difference between us. And this is what I have to put up with.

There was a solid clicking noise, then the jet's door swung up. Another click and the steps rolled down a moment later. Then, I lost track of my thoughts as the long lost brother of Lebron James got off the plane.

I don't actually know if Lebron James has a brother, and, if he does, he's probably not lost. But you get my drift.

Easily six feet tall and solidly built, Captain Devonte Moore looked like he had been chiseled from a block of stone, then patiently polished to perfection. With skin the color of chocolate and smooth as silk, he made me suddenly self-conscious of every callus and blemish on my body.

"Oh, momma," I mumbled involuntarily.

Isa dug an elbow into my side.

"Just part of the full service package we offer here at Blue Bullet Air," she said.

Given his near physical perfection, I wondered how Moore ended up flying for a tiny airline that was riding the the edge of bankruptcy instead of modeling for GQ magazine. I was also forced to ask if he just looked good, or could he actually fly the plane?

Isa gave me a little watch this  nod and said, "Give her the rundown, Captain."

"Yes, ma'am," Moore said, and then launched into a highlighted version of his resume.

A 2002 graduate of the United States Air Force Academy, he then spent six months in Nevada attending the Air Force Weapons School. That was followed by four years as a fighter pilot in Afghanistan, then two more years at Ellis Air Force Base in Nevada - this time on the instructor side - before he left the military.

"'Weapons School' sounds like a big deal," I said.

He shrugged that off, so Isa answered for him.

"Reserved for only the best and brightest," she said.

"Something like that... I'd imagine they had quite a career planned for you," I said.

Moore shook his head.

"Military wasn't really my thing," he said. "Too many rules."

I nodded, finding the sentiment easy to relate to.

"Then what?" I asked.

"I did some time with a small air taxi outfit in Japan," he said. "When they folded up, I made my way back home to California. Lot of small operations there."

"No commercial work?"

"Same as with the military," he said. "Just not my thing."

"And how'd you find your way out here?"

He stole a quick - but blatantly transparent - look at Isa and said, "Just lucky, I guess."

Ah-ha, I thought as I rolled my gaze to my friend.

"You know, you can't claim full service  when the product isn't actually available."

"He's for display purposes only," she said. "I didn't promise you could take him home."

Moore glanced back and forth between us and said, "You two know I'm standing right here, right?"

We all laughed, then I took a quick look at my watch.

"We gotta get going," I said. "Isa, I'll see you when we get back. Have the bill, or you're dead."

"Whatever."

"Captain," I continued, "let's get this show on the road."

"Yes, ma'am," he said as he gestured me aboard.

"You can knock off that ma'am  shit," I said as I climbed in.

He climbed in behind me and pulled the steps back up into place.

"It's sort of a pilot thing," he said.

"There's only two of us here," I said. "There's no need for a show. Plus, it annoys me."

He sighed, then - while looking outside at Isa - he said, "You two used to know each other back in the day, right?"

I didn't know exactly what she'd told him about the day  and I wasn't about to give away any of her secrets, so I simply said, "What makes you think that?"

"Oh, I don't know," he said as he pulled the door down and closed the plane. "You can just sorta tell."
