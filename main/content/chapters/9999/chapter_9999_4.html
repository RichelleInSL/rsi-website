Camp Slider was located right on the line that separates civilization from the bayou in southern Louisiana, and it was basically like any summer camp, anywhere. An entrance road that went on forever and transitioned from asphalt to gravel about halfway along the way led up to a small dirt and gravel parking lot beside a large main lodge. Though obviously a modern, split-log construction, it had been well-designed to maintain a classic, rustic appeal. A covered wooden deck extended off the front and around the side, skirted by formed concrete walls hidden beneath a clean and simple stonework facade. Above the steps hung a large banner, with the words "Welcome Campers" printed in lettering made to look like logs.

The lodge was the center of everything, serving as welcome center, meeting space, art studio and dining hall as needed throughout each session. Behind it snaked a trio of well-worn paths. A wooden frame had been planted in the ground at the point where they diverged, from which hung a series of charmingly simple directional signs. The first was labeled "BOYS CABINS (1-7)" with an arrow pointing left. The next indicated "GIRLS CABINS (8-13)" were to the right. The remaining signs all pointed down the center path, which meandered gently through the cypresses and tupelos and down to the lake. The swimming area was framed out by ropes and a T-shaped dock. To the right a shabby boathouse full of dented and weary-looking aluminum rowboats and canoes stood beside a small dock where a handful of small fiberglass sailboats bobbed lazily. Further right yet another dock was reserved for fishing and set well outside of casting range.

Other paths curled throughout the grounds. Wandering offshoots from the main trail, each offering its own unique view of the vegetation and wildlife the area had to offer, though the annual favorite was always Slider Walk. The trail - like the camp itself - took its name from the red-eared slider turtles that were common to the region. Much of the trail consisted of a long wooden deck built over the swampiest part of the camp, and the turtles were seemingly everywhere. Swimming in the muddy water, creeping along the shores and even sunning themselves on craggy rocks and downed trees.

Life was everywhere in the bayou. Gators, otters, egrets, cranes and the occasional wild boar were regular sights among the campers. But if it was turtles you wanted, Slider Walk was the place to be. And everyone wanted to see the turtles.

The turtles, Danni would one day think to herself, were really where all the trouble started.
